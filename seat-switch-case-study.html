Context

The brief came with a screenshot of a competitor's feature already attached

The PRD's design reference wasn't a wireframe — it was two screenshots of MakeMyTrip's existing "Confirmed Option" feature, captioned simply "Wireframes: MMT Design Screenshots." The literal ask was close to: build what they already have.

I treated that as a floor, not a brief. Before opening Figma, I ran an independent problem-framing pass — my own success metrics, my own risk list, my own read on what would make a user actually trust this — and only then went looking at what MMT had gotten right and wrong.

The Problem

A confirmed seat can exist and still be invisible to the person looking for it

redRail's search results page only ever showed a direct journey as a single seat, in a single class, for the whole route. If that didn't exist, the user saw Waitlist — even when the train itself had room, just not in one contiguous block.

"In many cases, seats are available in different classes for different parts of the journey on the same train, but our current system does not allow users to discover or book these combinations. This results in a lost opportunity for both the user and the business." — Product Requirements Document, Problem Statement
Three ways it shows up
01

High drop-off

No direct confirmed ticket meant users resorted to manually piecing together separate bookings — cumbersome enough that most simply left the search results page.

02

Lost opportunity

Partial-leg inventory in other classes existed on the very train being searched, but nothing in the product could surface or sell it.

03

Trust erosion

Failing to find a seat that technically existed is a worse experience than not having one — users switch platforms rather than keep trying.

Research

MMT had already solved discovery. It hadn't solved booking.

Before concepting, I read MMT's flow as a user would, end to end, rather than just tracing its layout.

Track 1 — competitive teardown

What MMT's "Confirmed Option" actually does

  • The discovery moment works: a clear badge on the search tuple pulls attention to the option.
  • The booking moment doesn't: each leg has its own standalone "BOOK" button. The user manually completes one purchase, then has to remember to come back and do the second.
  • No visible mechanism ties the two bookings together as one trip once you're past the detail screen.
Track 2 — my own framing pass

What the PRD didn't hand me

Before sketching anything, I ran a structured board: problem framing, my own success metrics, a risk list, and a question the requirements doc never asked: what would actually make a user willing to interact with this?

  • Named the psychological mechanism directly: relief of seeing green "Available" replace red "Waitlist" is the actual hook.
  • Surfaced physical-world risk early — luggage, short halts, elderly travelers — none of which appear in the PRD's requirements table.
  • Listed the exact asset inventory the feature would touch across the app before a single screen was drawn.

What I carried into concepting: keep the one thing MMT got right — a legible discovery cue on the search results tuple — and fix the thing it didn't: make the two bookings behave as one transaction. That single decision became FR-04 in the PRD, the requirement that most separates this from the pattern it was modeled on.

Framing the Goals

Three jobs, in the order a traveller actually experiences them

1

Get noticed, without noise

Surface the option on a search results page that's already dense with fares, quotas, and tags — without it reading as another badge to ignore.

2

Book like it's one trip

One passenger form, one payment, two PNRs generated automatically behind it — not two separate booking journeys stitched together by the user.

3

Earn trust in the switch

A traveller has to believe — before paying — that changing seats at an intermediate station on the same train is safe, simple, and clearly explained.

The direction that shipped folded the feature into redRail's existing naming pattern (the same convention used for "redRail wallet") instead of inventing a new voice. It drops the narrative entirely and lets two hard facts — ticket count, switch station — do the work. Restraint won over cleverness.

Scoping — Phase 2

Extending the inventory logic without changing what the user sees

Once baseline Seat Switch (Same Train Seat Switch, or STSS) shipped, the follow-on phase asked a harder question: what if the exact two-leg split still doesn't have usable availability, but a slightly longer booked stretch on one leg would?

"When my preferred train has no confirmable seat on my exact S–D, I want a same-train booking plan — including cases where one or both ticket segments are route-extended — so I can travel that train with a mid-journey seat switch and a higher chance of confirmation." — Job to be done, Route Extension expansion

Cluster (TBS + TBS_RE)Mixed — one exact stretch, one route-extended leg
What stays the same

One train, two tickets, one seat change

The traveller-facing mental model doesn't change at all, whatever the backend logic behind it.

What absorbs the complexity

Clear board/alight callouts

A booked segment running longer than the traveller's real journey is handled on top of the existing two-leg pattern, not a new UI paradigm.

Per the PRD, baseline two-leg STSS was the scope already live; Route Extension and cluster-station variants were the scope this phase defined to bring live next.

Systems Thinking

Priority logic and edge cases, documented before build

Not every combination of leg availability is worth surfacing. The priority table below governs which combinations the matching engine offers first — legs 1 and 2 are treated as interchangeable.

PriorityLeg 1Leg 2
1Available / RACAvailable / RAC
2Available / RACWaitlist (with or without SG)
3Waitlist (with/without SG)Waitlist (with/without SG)

Non-functional constraints that shaped the UI, not just the backend:

SRP load time budget≤ 3s, even with Seat Switch matching logic running (NFR-01)
Search/booking volume headroom+50% without degradation (NFR-02)
Booking reliability target> 99.5% success rate (NFR-03)
Limitations, scoped and stated up front — not discovered in QA
Phase 1 supports exactly two journey legs — no chained, multi-switch itineraries.
Connecting journeys composed as Switch + Switch are explicitly out of scope for the Route Extension phase.
No changes to IRCTC quota, charting, or seat-allotment rules — the feature composes existing inventory, it doesn't create new rules.
Same berth type or coach continuity across the switch is not guaranteed.
No single-PNR "virtual" switch — two real PNRs are a deliberate, IRCTC-compliant constraint, not a shortcut we chose not to take.
Edge cases named early and scoped for a later pass: only one leg confirming, add-ons applied to both legs, dual senior-citizen quota, and a dedicated "how this works" explainer.

Reflection

What I'd carry into the next one

The minimal version of this project was sitting in the brief: port MMT's badge-and-detail-sheet pattern, wire it to the six functional requirements, ship it. That would have shipped a working feature.

What made it worth doing properly was running an independent problem-framing pass before touching the reference at all, reading the competitor's flow closely enough to find the one thing it hadn't solved, and then testing thirteen-plus variants of layout and copy before letting the calmest, most branded option win over the more narrative ones. The edge-case list — regret flows, dual quota, an explainer page — was mine to write, not the PRD's, and it's the backlog for the next pass. So is formalizing how General/Ladies/Tatkal ("GFT") quota interacts with a two-leg switch, which is currently scoped but not yet designed in detail.

The Route Extension phase is the clearest test of whether this held up: the underlying inventory logic got substantially more complex, and the user-facing pattern — one train, two tickets, one seat change — didn't have to change at all.
